# Fine-art advisory — personal identity

Role: cartoonist–fine artist advising the identity designer and art director.

## The useful tension

The site gives Jason space to be a person. Its identity should have the same generosity: sturdy enough to sign serious work, strange enough to belong to someone. Character comes from the relationship of a few shapes, not a pile of symbols for his interests.

## Criteria sent to the identity designer

1. **Recognize the silhouette first.** The mark must register as one thing before a viewer decodes initials or a concept. Cover any wordmark and remove the brand colors; it should still have a clear shape.
2. **Make one surprising decision.** An offset opening, unequal rhythm, or deliberate bend can supply character. Several quirks competing in one small mark make it feel arbitrary.
3. **Keep it small on purpose.** The primary mark must retain its identity at 16, 20, and 24 pixels, with no essential hairline, tiny enclosed counter, or fragile separate dot. The favicon may use an optical simplification if necessary.
4. **Avoid a literal inventory.** Eyes, sprouts, notebook pages, and quills are readily understood but too broadly associated with curiosity, growth, or thought. Do not combine them into an explanation of Jason. A direct structural idea is stronger.
5. **Let the drawing breathe.** A little asymmetry is welcome; simulated wobble or distressed edges are not a substitute for drawing. Uneven visual weight should feel intended, not unresolved.
6. **Separate the signature from the illustration.** The identity signs the page. The larger artwork is where the garden, music, workshop, and human life can coexist. Both can share contour weight or shape rhythm without sharing a literal subject.
7. **Use one-color proof.** Solid cocoa on cream and reversed cream on cocoa are the basic tests. Rust and lavender are supporting colors, not mechanisms required to understand the mark.
8. **Check the unintended picture.** Look for accidental corporate arrows, tools, religious signs, faces, or generic technology initials. Association alone is not disqualifying; it matters when it overwhelms the intended identity.

## Actual symbol review

Reviewed the vector construction of `branding/personal-v1/assets/open-thought-mark.svg`, the designer's candidate **The turn**, and its companion favicon. This review is of the actual SVG geometry; raster-size visual verification remains part of the final browser review.

**Recommendation: develop and use this direction.** One continuous filled shape joins an F's angular upper structure to a J's curved lower turn. It has the right economy: the initials can be discovered without a separate symbol explaining curiosity. Repeated diagonal terminals establish a clear rule; the rounded lower gesture interrupts that rule once. That controlled contrast supplies character without simulated hand drawing.

**Refinement requested directly from the designer:** balance the primary symbol's optical safe area. The initial path spans approximately x=2–81 and y=12–89 within a 96-square view box. The very small left margin makes the J terminal vulnerable at small display sizes. The favicon already insets the path with a transform, which helps; the primary asset should also have intentional placement.

**Size test:** the principal stem is 16 units wide in the initial 96-unit drawing, equivalent to about 2.7 pixels at a 16-pixel display. This is a promising foundation, but the middle arm, diagonal ends, and J opening need rendered inspection at 16/20/24 pixels. Test the favicon itself, not only the large logo scaled in a presentation.

**Restraint:** do not add a dot, leaf, eye, or second explanatory stroke. Keep “The turn” as a useful internal design name; the public identity does not need its meaning spelled out. Larger narrative illustration should remain separate from the small signature.
