A closer look / StatBatt

A number is a start.
Context is better.

A battery percentage tells you what’s left. I wanted a small Mac app that helps you understand what’s happening—and keeps its claims as clear as its interface.

My role
Product design, architecture & implementation
What I made
Native menu bar app, local history & charging workflow
Current state
Local development candidate · public landing live

Core technologies

  • Swift 6
  • SwiftUI / AppKit
  • IOKit / IOPowerSources
  • SQLite
  • Apple Shortcuts
01 / The question

What sits behind
that percentage?

A percentage is useful, but it leaves a lot unanswered. Is the Mac drawing from its battery or an external supply? What has changed over the last few hours? Which readings are measurements, which are estimates, and which simply aren’t available?

StatBatt brings those questions into a native menu bar app. The goal is a quiet place to check the present, inspect a little history, and make an informed choice without turning battery care into another dashboard to manage.

02 / The decisions

Keep the unknowns
in the picture.

Missing information stays missing. A sleeping Mac leaves a gap in the history rather than a reassuring line through a period the app never observed. Health and pack temperature are labeled as estimates when available, and advanced readings depend on the device and macOS version.

History stays in a local SQLite database, with seven-day retention and CSV export. People can pause or clear it. Notifications and launch-at-login are opt-in. Closing the detail window leaves monitoring in the menu bar; the app doesn’t need to keep asking for attention.

03 / What I built

A small surface.
Clear boundaries underneath.

The SwiftUI interface brings together Battery, Charging, History, and Settings views. macOS power-source notifications and a fallback sampler supply the readings; sleep and wake handling prevent old observations from passing as current ones.

Separate Swift modules handle telemetry, persistence, domain rules, and the native charging workflow. That separation makes it possible to test decisions with synthetic inputs while keeping hardware support tied to observed behavior on a real machine.

The local build offers deliberate 80% and 100% actions through trusted Apple Shortcuts. It records intent before dispatch and does not replay a charging request at startup. If an outcome is uncertain, the app asks for reconciliation instead of assuming success. Apple owns the resulting setting, which persists after StatBatt quits.

04 / The proof

A passing test.
A separate hardware question.

The project’s verification records cover telemetry decoding, local storage, missing readings, sleep gaps, and charging-request recovery. The local app has been built and packaged, and both charge-limit buttons were exercised through the app and checked against Apple’s Battery settings on the tested Apple silicon configuration.

Those are different kinds of evidence. Software tests can check a decision or a recovery rule; they cannot establish electrical cutoff behavior or compatibility with every Mac. Keeping that boundary explicit is part of the engineering, not just a note at the end.

05 / Where it stands

Working locally.
Still earning its release.

Monitoring, local history, export, preferences, and the native charge-limit workflow are implemented in the local candidate. Charging support is limited to the tested configuration. Custom charge bands, intentional discharge, and automatic temperature-based control remain unavailable.

There is no signed and notarized public download yet. Broader hardware and lifecycle checks, accessibility verification, and distribution work remain ahead. The public landing page introduces the project with sample readings; StatBatt is proprietary software from Orca Solutions.
