A closer look / GetFit

A plan for the work.
A record of what happened.

I wanted a workout app that kept my logs, explained its plans, and let me change the rules. So I built a personal tool—and made the source available for someone else to adapt.

01 / The question

What if the log
came first?

I started with a practical frustration: losing workout records in an app I depended on. The replacement needed to save each set as I entered it, remain useful offline, and give me a way to take the data elsewhere.

GetFit is personal software with a focused job. It suggests the next workout, records what I actually do, and keeps the difference visible. The interface is plain because the work is in the planning and the record.

02 / The decisions

Periodization with
rules you can read.

The generator creates four-week blocks: three loading weeks followed by a deload. Heavy, moderate, and light sessions rotate across the lifting days, with different rep ranges and more exercise variety on lighter days. The deload reduces the planned work.

Equipment availability shapes exercise selection. Repeated movements provide continuity, while the planner can rotate selections when logged history indicates a stall. Each workout carries a short rationale, so its structure is visible rather than hidden behind a score.

The generator’s rules are explicit TypeScript, so someone adapting the app can follow a decision from the inputs to the resulting workout—and change it.

03 / What I built

Suggestions above.
Records underneath.

The workout screen separates planned sets from actual entries and can show previous loads at matching rep ranges. Logged history feeds progression suggestions, including different handling for added weight and assistance. Calendar and history views make it possible to inspect the plan alongside the work recorded.

React and TypeScript provide the interface. The planning logic lives separately from the screens. Dexie stores records in the browser’s IndexedDB; a Hono API and SQLite provide the sync layer. A PWA service worker supports the app’s offline use, and JSON import and export keep the records portable.

04 / Make it yours

Personal by origin.
Open to adaptation.

I built GetFit around my own needs, rather than a general service with accounts and subscriptions. Its public repository carries an MIT license, so another person can reuse, modify, and build on the code.

The exercise catalog, equipment profile, and generation rules give someone a starting point for their own version. The value is both a working application and a readable implementation of the decisions that produce a workout.
